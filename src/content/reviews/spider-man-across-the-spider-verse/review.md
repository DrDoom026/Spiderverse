If the first *Spider-Verse* film was a love letter to the idea that anyone can wear the mask, then *Across the Spider-Verse* is the moment the sequel earns the right to complicate that promise. It is bigger, more ambitious, more visually reckless than its predecessor — and it is very nearly as good. The word "nearly" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, because this film does things that animated cinema has not dared to attempt before.

## The Canon Event

The film's central philosophical question — whether certain events must happen for a Spider-Man to *be* Spider-Man — is one of the most interesting ideas to appear in mainstream blockbuster cinema in years. The Spider-Society's Spot database of "canon events" (the death of a mentor, a loss that defines the hero) functions as a metaphor for trauma as identity, for the way grief becomes load-bearing architecture in a person's sense of self.

Miguel O'Hara, voiced with volcanic intensity by Oscar Isaac, represents the logical endpoint of this philosophy taken too far: a man so committed to the canon that he has become a tyrant enforcing narrative law. He is the sequel's most interesting creation, and the film is smart enough to make him genuinely persuasive before revealing the cost of his worldview.

## Six Studios, One Film

The visual ambition of *Across the Spider-Verse* eclipses even its predecessor, which is a staggering achievement. Each universe has its own artistic DNA: Mumbattan blends Indian miniature painting with mechanical spider-web geometry; Hobie Brown's punk London is a torn-collage of newspaper clippings and spray paint; the Spider-Society HQ rotates between seventeen distinct art styles as characters from different dimensions interact.

The craft required to make this coherent — to make it *feel* unified rather than chaotic — is extraordinary. And largely it succeeds.

## Miles in Crisis

Shameik Moore's vocal performance as Miles deepens considerably from the first film. This Miles is more confident on the surface, and more frightened underneath. The film's most devastating sequence — in which he is told, in front of an auditorium of Spider-People, that he was never supposed to be bitten — lands with the weight of a hammer blow precisely because we have spent two films loving this character.

## The Verdict

*Across the Spider-Verse* is an unfinished masterpiece, and I mean that in the most literal sense: it ends mid-story, a deliberate cliffhanger that trusts its audience will return. That bet feels safe. This is cinema that reminds you what the medium can do when it stops being cautious.

**Nine out of ten. Impatiently awaiting the conclusion.**
